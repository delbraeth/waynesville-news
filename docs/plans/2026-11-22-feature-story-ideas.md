# Planning note: local feature story ideas (2026-11-22)

**Status:** Idea list from the editor and Claude, 2026-11-22 (topics 20–25 added the same day). Nothing here is
verified yet. Details marked "(verify)" are from memory: check every fact
against the organization's own page, an original source, or a source the editor

**How to publish one:** write it as a story in `src/content/stories/` with
`feature: true`, so it goes to Facebook with the dark "Feature" header (DEPLOY.md §6).
Publishing a new story needs the editor's go-ahead. Label lighthearted pieces
as lighthearted. Never name private living people. The editor approved

## Founding and namesakes

1. **Who was "Mad" Anthony Wayne?** The Revolutionary War general behind the
   village and township names: the nickname, his 1794 win at Fallen Timbers,
   and the 1795 Treaty of Greenville that opened the area to settlement.
   Sources: Ohio History Connection, National Park Service.
2. **How Waynesville began (1797).** The founding and who platted the town
   (an English settler, verify), why Main Street looks the way it does, and the
   historic district's National Register listing. Sources: Waynesville
   Historical Society, the National Register nomination.
3. **The names behind the names.** Corwin, Harveysburg, Oregonia, Crosswick,
   Caesar Creek (the origin story is debated, so handle it carefully), and Mary L.
   Cook, the library's namesake.

## Quaker roots and freedom

4. **Waynesville's Quakers.** The Miami Monthly Meeting, Friends Home (now a
   museum) and the settlers who shaped the village. Source: Museum at the
   Friends Home.
5. **The Underground Railroad locally.** Only documented sites and people,
   with legend clearly separated from the record. A good honest angle: the
   written record is thin because people kept quiet to stay safe, even after the
   war, so the story is what's known, what's claimed, and how historians tell
   them apart. Pair with #6 for February.
   **Research so far (2026-11-22):**
   - **Confirmed, NPS Network to Freedom:** Waynesville's listing is a
     *program*, not a site. It is "Waynesville's Role in the Underground
     Railroad" (the NPS dataset spells it "Wayneville's"), ID 27OH04, accepted
     2014. Designed by the Waynesville Underground Railroad Committee (WURC)
     and first presented in 2012, it is a yearly program for Waynesville
     Middle School sixth graders: a PowerPoint lecture, a walking tour, and a
     living-history reenactment at Camp Joy near Clarksville. Per the NPS
     abstract, it draws on the 1840 Miami Monthly Meeting minutes and letters
     in the Wilbur Siebert Collection, and says the village was settled by
     Quakers from the Bush River (South Carolina) Meeting. No Waynesville
     *site* is listed. Source: the NPS Network to Freedom listings dataset (CSV,
     updated 2026-10-09), nps.gov/subjects/undergroundrailroad/ntf-listings.htm.
     Follow-up: is the program still running? Ask Wayne Local and the WURC
     (organizations only).
   - **Miami Monthly Meeting, "Miami Friends and Slavery"**
     (quaker.org/legacy/miami/index_files/slavery.html): says "many Quaker
     Underground Railroad sites were located in Waynesville, Springboro, and
     Harveysburg" (no sites named, no citations); Butterworth Farm near
     Maineville was "a resting place for escaped slaves," with a 2015 Ohio
     Historical Marker for "Butterworth Station, Warren County's southernmost
     station." It notes some Friends struggled with the secrecy, given their
     testimony of truth. Butterworth is not in the NPS list.
   - **Primary sources to pursue:** the 1840 Miami Monthly Meeting minutes,
     and the Wilbur Siebert Collection (Ohio History Connection; Siebert
     gathered Underground Railroad accounts in the 1890s).
   - **Claimed, unconfirmed:** Thomas Arnett and Achilles Pugh as Waynesville
     abolitionists (Pugh said to have aided freedom seekers after moving here
     in 1854); the "Haines House, 108 Second Street" as a station. **Caution:**
     the NPS-listed Haines House (08OH01, 2004) is in **Alliance, Stark
     County**, not Waynesville, and the "Haines House Underground Railroad
     Museum" web pages are likely about Alliance. Don't mix them up.
   - **A certificate at the Museum at the Friends Home** from NPS and the
     Freedom Center is reported; it probably relates to the 2014 program
     listing. Confirm with the museum.
   - **Rumor only:** Marble Hall at Otterbein.
6. **Harveysburg's school for Black children (1831).** Often called Ohio's
   first free school for Black children (verify the wording). Source: the
   Harveysburg Community Historical Society.

## Lost places

7. **New Burlington, the village under Caesar Creek Lake.** Cleared for the
   Army Corps of Engineers' dam in the 1970s, and the subject of John Baskin's
   1976 book (verify). Could be a two-part series.
   **Lake timeline (leads, confirm with the Corps' Louisville District or
   ODNR):** authorized in the Flood Control Act of 1938; the Corps began the dam
   in 1971 and finished it in 1978; ODNR dedicated the state park (about 4,700
   acres) and wildlife area (about 2,500 acres) in 1978. The lake is about 2,830
   acres, on land the state leases from the Corps. Sources so far: Wikipedia,
   International Parks, Recreation.gov. **2028 is the lake's 50th
   anniversary,** a natural time for a New Burlington and Caesar Creek Lake
   series (the 40 years from authorization to dam, what was lost, and what the lake became).
8. **Caesar Creek Pioneer Village.** The log buildings that were saved and moved
   there, and who keeps it going.

## Deep history

9. **Fort Ancient.** The hilltop earthworks near Oregonia, part of the
   Hopewell Ceremonial Earthworks World Heritage Site (verify the 2023
   inscription). What "Fort Ancient" means, and what it doesn't.
10. **450-million-year-old fossils at the Caesar Creek spillway.** Why the
    spot is famous, and the permit rules for collecting. Sources: ODNR, Army Corps.
11. **The Little Miami, a scenic river.** Its early state and national
    Scenic River designations (verify the years), and the rail trail through
    Corwin that was once a railroad.

## The Waynesville pearl rush

26. **Freshwater pearls and the Harris collection.** Pearls from the Little
    Miami and local creeks set off a pearl rush. Waynesville banker Israel H.
    Harris built one of the country's best-known collections of river pearls,
    which was shown at the 1889 Paris World's Fair. Part of it is reportedly at
    the Smithsonian, and part is at the Museum at the Friends Home. The ending:
    pollution and overharvesting finished off the river's pearls.
    **Checked against the primary source (2026-11-22):** George F. Kunz, "The
    Fresh-Water Pearls and Pearl Fisheries of the United States," *Bulletin
    of the United States Fish Commission* (volume for 1897), p. 394
    (spo.nmfs.noaa.gov/sites/default/files/pdf-content/fish-bull/fb17.41.pdf;
    the PDF is 13 MB, so download it and extract the text). Kunz was Tiffany's gem expert. He says:
    - about 1868, "many fine pearls were obtained from Little Miami River, Ohio";
    - "Some of the finest American pearls that were next found came from near
      Waynesville, Ohio, **$3,000 worth** being collected in that vicinity during
      the pearl excitement of **1876**";
    - Harris then began "one of the finest and best-known collections of Unio
      pearls in this country," buying "every specimen of value" in the area;
      it held "more than 2,000 pearls, weighing over 2,000 grains," including a
      38-grain button-shaped pearl, several "almost transparent pink ones," and
      a pearl that had grown almost through the shell; "in all probability the
      last collection that will be made from that district";
    - it "was exhibited in the jewelry department at the World's Fair held in
      Paris during 1889."
    Kunz says **nothing about a Tiffany purchase or the Smithsonian.**
    **Conflicts to resolve before writing:**
    - The Warren County Post (2022) says about **$25,000** in pearls was found in
      **1878**; Kunz says **$3,000** in **1876**. Use Kunz unless a better source turns up.
    - The Post (quoting the Friends Home museum) says part of the 1889 exhibit
      won a gold medal. Kunz doesn't mention a medal, so it's unconfirmed.
    - The Tiffany 1888 purchase appears only in secondary web sources and is
      unconfirmed.
    - The Smithsonian holding is the museum's statement, unconfirmed. The
      Smithsonian collections search (collections.si.edu) blocks automated
      access, so check it in a browser (search "I. H. Harris" and "Waynesville").
    **Friends Home collection:** donated by Harris's great-granddaughter, per
    the Warren County Post (2022), which quotes the museum calling it the
    second-largest collection of Harris pearls after the Smithsonian's. Ask the
    museum how many pearls it holds and for photos.
    **Bonus thread:** Kunz also describes river-mussel shells, drilled and
    carved, from Taylor's mound at Oregonia in Warren County, and pearls
    found in mounds of the Little Miami Valley (the Turner group). That could tie in
    with #9 (Fort Ancient).
    A strong standalone feature at any time of year; could pair with #11 (the Little Miami) or a museum visit.

## More hidden-history leads (found 2026-11-22, all unverified)

27. **The *Miami Visitor*, Waynesville's abolitionist newspaper (1850–1861).**
    A weekly whose publisher, J. Wesley Roberts, is described as an
    abolitionist and temperance advocate; it is said to have run from Jan. 30,
    1850, to Jan. 23, 1861, ending on the eve of the Civil War. It pairs with #5.
    Look for surviving issues (Ohio History Connection, Chronicling America, the
    Mary L. Cook library). Source so far: local history listings.
28. **I. H. Harris, banker.** Waynesville's bank reportedly opened a
    short-lived branch in Lebanon in 1877, after Lebanon's own bank closed.
    Check whether that was Harris's bank, which would make a natural sidebar to #26 (the pearls).
29. **Stagecoach days and the Hammel House.** Sources say a log tavern stood
    on the site before the present building, which was an inn and stagecoach stop
    on the People's Stage Line (dates conflict: 1799, 1817, 1822). Enoch Hammel
    owned it from 1841 to 1863, which is where the name comes from. Keep the
    history (stage routes, travelers, the National Road era) separate from the
    ghost stories, which belong in #14. Sources: 1882 *History of Warren
    County*, the Warren County GenWeb transcriptions, the Friends Home museum's
    house histories.
30. **The founder's ambitions.** Samuel Heighway, an Englishman from
    Shropshire, founded the town in 1797 (some sources say laid out in 1796,
    platted in 1802 "in the style of an English village"), on land bought
    through John Cleves Symmes. A local tourism history says he hoped it would
    become the territorial capital; other sources don't mention this, so treat it as
    tradition. This could fold into #2.
31. **The 1881 Engine House and Lockup.** The village's old firehouse and
    jail, listed on the National Register in 1999. A short "building with a
    story" piece. Source: the National Register nomination (via NPS).
32. **Eli Harvey (1860–1957), Quaker animal sculptor.** Born in Ogden,
    Clinton County (not Waynesville), but the Mary L. Cook library keeps a file
    on him among "Famous Quakers from SW Ohio." He became one of the country's
    leading animal sculptors, known for the Elks lodge elk among other works;
    the Smithsonian American Art Museum has an artist page for him. Only a
    regional tie, so it may suit a "Quaker neighbors" piece more than a Waynesville
    story. Verify any family link to the Harveys of Harveysburg before claiming one.
33. **The Mary L. Cook library's local-history files** are a gold mine for
    all of these: mlcook.lib.oh.us/content/waynesville-history (including
    "Harveysburg on Caesar's Creek" and the Eli Harvey file). Also the
    Waynesville genealogy blog (waynesgenhis.blogspot.com) on old houses and
    families, and the 1882 *History of Warren County* (Wayne Township
    chapter, transcribed online).

34. **Lt. Suella Bernard of Waynesville, a WWII flight nurse.**
    **Confirmed (Stars and Stripes, London edition, June 12, 1944; reprinted
    unedited June 7, 2024, stripes.com/history/2024-06-07/us-army-nurses-land-d-day-14088869.html):**
    "2/Lt. ... Suella Bernard, of Waynesville, Ohio" was one of five Army
    nurses who, on Saturday, June 10, 1944 (four days after D-Day), "became the
    first Allied women to land in France." With a Ninth Air Force
    evacuation unit (a doctor, six enlisted technicians and the nurses),
    they flew C-47s from England to dirt airstrips in the Cherbourg peninsula beachhead and
    evacuated 14 stretcher cases, including wounded prisoners, back to Britain
    the same day (took off 8:49 a.m., back by 1:10 p.m.). The other nurses were
    from Columbus, Ohio; Kersey, Pa.; St. Petersburg, Fla.; and Cornwall,
    Conn. The 1944 article uses a wartime slur for a Japanese prisoner, so
    don't quote that passage.
    **Also:** the National Museum of the U.S. Air Force (Dayton) is honoring
    "1st Lt. Suella Bernard of Waynesville, who cared for wounded soldiers
    during a glider evacuation mission in Germany," in its November 2026
    "Ohio Serves" (America 250-Ohio) gallery displays, per Vintage Aviation
    News (Nov. 8, 2026). Not yet checked on the museum's own site. The glider
    mission in Germany is a separate, later mission (possibly the spring 1945
    glider medical evacuations, unconfirmed), and the promotion to 1st Lt.
    fits a later date.
    **Timely hook:** the museum display is up now (November 2026), so this could run
    soon as a short item ("A Waynesville nurse is honored at the Air Force
    museum") with the full feature on the **June 10, 2027** anniversary.
    **To find:** her later life, family ties and burial (only details already
    public: obituaries, cemetery records, the Friends Home museum, the Mary L.
    Cook library; no living relatives' names without the editor's OK); her
    squadron (likely a Medical Air Evacuation Transport Squadron); the
    glider mission details; and any photos the Air Force museum or the National
    Archives can share.

**Not usable yet:** a regional "notables" page lists Phyllis McGuire (the McGuire
Sisters), but nothing ties her to Waynesville, so leave it out.

## Festivals and fun

12. **History of the Ohio Sauerkraut Festival.** From a small village event
    to one of Ohio's biggest festivals: the first year (1970?, verify),
    founding stories, the strangest kraut foods. Source: festival organizers.
    Best timing: October, the week before the festival.
13. **How Waynesville became an antiques town.** When the shops took over
    Main Street, and the longest-running ones.
14. **The "most haunted village in Ohio."** Where the reputation came from,
    the ghost walks, and which buildings have stories. Light in tone, for Halloween.
15. **The Ohio Renaissance Festival in Harveysburg.** How a medieval village
    was built in a Warren County field.
16. **Red Stewart Airfield and the air show.** The grass runway, the skydivers,
    and the family behind it (verify).
17. **Fox Hollow Rodeo.** How long it has run and who runs it.

20. **History of the Warren County Fair.** How far back it goes (verify the
    first year), how it came to the Lebanon fairgrounds, 4-H and the junior
    fair, and old traditions and oddities. Source: the Warren County
    Agricultural Society or fair board, and the historical society. Best timing:
    the week before the July fair.
21. **The gliderport.** The Caesar Creek Soaring Club's gliderport near
    Waynesville (verify the name, location and founding year): how gliders fly
    without engines, who flies them, and whether visitors can take a ride.
    Source: the club. Best timing: spring or summer.

## Getting outside (a possible "Out and about" series)

22. **Hiking Caesar Creek.** A trail guide: the long perimeter loop, the
    shorter family hikes, the Caesar Creek Gorge State Nature Preserve and the
    best spots for spring wildflowers or fall color (verify trail names and
    distances). Sources: ODNR, Army Corps, Ohio DNR Natural Areas.
    Best timing: April or October.
23. **Horse trails.** Caesar Creek's bridle trails and horseman's camp (verify
    the mileage and camp details), the riding community, and trail etiquette
    for hikers and riders sharing paths. Ties in with the horse parades and
    the rodeo. Sources: ODNR, local riding groups (organizations only).
24. **Paddling and biking the Little Miami.** Canoe liveries and the scenic
    rail trail through Corwin, as a companion to #11.
25. **Spring Valley Lake and the wildlife area.** The lake and marsh at the
    Spring Valley Wildlife Area, just north of Waynesville near the
    Greene–Warren county line (verify the acreage and which agency manages it,
    likely ODNR Division of Wildlife). Birding (migrating waterfowl, herons),
    the boardwalk or marsh trail (verify), fishing, and its link to the Little
    Miami Scenic Trail. Sources: ODNR, local birding groups (organizations only).
    Best timing: spring or fall migration.
    **Leads (confirm with the ODNR Division of Wildlife):** land purchases
    for the public hunting and fishing area began in 1953; the lake came from
    improving the old Sinclair Fur Farm lake and marsh in 1954 (Birding
    Hotspots, birdinghotspots.org/group/G234687407). Sources give the size as
    an 80-acre lake or a 150-acre lake-and-marsh complex. A 2.5-mile trail
    circles the lake; the fish are largemouth bass, bluegill, bullhead and carp
    (Dayton Daily News). An Outdoor News item mentions an ODNR marsh and dam
    renovation (date unclear). A historical marker is at the site
    (hmdb.org/m.asp?m=254402).
